# Your data: who sees it

Before you paste a herd export or a staff roster into a chat tool, spend a minute on where that text goes. Usually the answer is fine. Sometimes you'll change one setting or leave a few columns out.

> **On the farm:** Farm records carry your business details and often other people's: staff phone numbers, leave and health notes. You're responsible for some of that under the Privacy Act, whichever tool you paste it into.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Find the data settings in the AI tool you use and explain how personal and business accounts differ.
- Use the NZ Farm Data Code to ask a farm software provider who has rights to your data.
- Cut staff details down to what the job needs, knowing that removing names isn't always enough.
- Get weather from measured data and your forecast provider, and use AI to explain it.

## What happens to what you paste

Anything you paste or upload goes to the provider's servers to be processed. The chat usually stays in your history until you delete it. On some personal accounts, your chats can also be used to improve the provider's future models unless you switch that off.

Menu names change, so in ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, Google Gemini or Claude, open Settings and find the data, privacy or activity section. Check whether your chats can be used to train the models, and how long your history is kept.

Business accounts differ. If you sign in to Copilot with a work Microsoft 365 login, or use a business or team plan of the others, the terms usually say your content isn't used for training by default. The same tool can follow different rules on each plan, so read the terms for the one you're on.

Then trim what you paste. Does the tool need names, or would roles do? Does it need the whole file, or only the columns your question is about? Passwords, bank details and login codes never go into a chat.

## Who can use farm data: the NZ Farm Data Code

Your herd and pasture records also live with the companies whose apps you use. The [NZ Farm Data Code of Practice](https://www.farmdatacode.org.nz/) is a voluntary code for organisations that collect, store and share farm data. Accredited organisations agree to tell you three things: who has rights to the data they hold, how it's processed and shared, and how it's stored and secured. The code's website lists which organisations are accredited.

Those make good questions for any farm app, accredited or not:

```text
Who owns the data I enter, and who owns reports you build from it?
Do you share my data with anyone else, and can I say no?
Can I export all my records, and delete them if I leave?
Where is my data stored?
```

General AI chat tools have their own terms, so check those directly. One handy use: paste a provider's data policy into a chat and ask the AI to answer those four questions, quoting the policy wording for each. Then read the quoted parts yourself.

## Staff information and the Privacy Act

The Privacy Commissioner's starting point is that [the Privacy Act applies to everyone using AI tools in New Zealand](https://www.privacy.org.nz/resources-and-learning/a-z-topics/ai/). Its [privacy principles](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-principles/) cover how you collect, store, use and share personal information, which on a farm mostly means staff details.

If you collected Tom's phone number to roster him on, that's what it's for. Pasting it into a personal AI account with his leave reasons and a note about his back injury goes further than a roster needs.

> **Important:** Keep staff health, disciplinary and personal contact details out of personal AI accounts. If the roster only needs names and days, give it names and days.

### Removing names isn't always anonymous

On a farm with four staff, "the 2IC who started in July" is as good as a name. Tag numbers and kill dates can point straight at your business. Swapping names for numbers doesn't help if the other details still make someone obvious, so drop the details the question doesn't need as well.

## Weather: measured, forecast, explained

An AI chat tool isn't a weather service. It may have no current data, and if it searches the web you still need to check its source. For weather you act on, go to the source yourself:

- **Measured:** your own gauge, plus a nearby climate station or your regional council's rainfall sites when your gauge has a gap
- **Forecast:** your provider directly, such as [MetService's rural forecasts](https://www.metservice.com/rural), noting the issue time

AI helps with the explaining. Copy the forecast text in and ask for the parts you care about:

```text
Here's the rural forecast I copied at 6 am, and my gauge readings for the week.
Tell me what the forecast says about rain and wind for Thursday, quoting its words.
Keep my measured readings separate. Don't add anything that isn't in the text.
```

For spraying or shifting stock ahead of a storm, go by the provider's forecast and warnings and what you see on the day. The AI's summary is a reading aid.

## Key takeaways

- Check your tool's data settings before you paste. Business plans usually have different terms from personal ones.
- Ask any farm app who has rights to your data and how it's shared and stored. The NZ Farm Data Code gives you the questions.
- The Privacy Act applies to staff details you put into AI tools. Give the tool only what the job needs.
- Removing names isn't enough if other details still identify someone.
- Get measured weather from gauges and stations and forecasts from the provider. Use AI to explain them.

You can still use AI on your farm records every week. Spend a minute choosing what goes in, and keep the private bits on the farm.

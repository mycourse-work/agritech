# Check before you use it

The draft came back in three seconds and it reads well. It also says Dave is booked in for 8 am Tuesday. Nobody booked Dave.

> **On the farm:** A wrong time in a text to a contractor costs you a wasted morning and an awkward phone call. A wrong date in a summary of your resource consent can cost a lot more. The check takes a couple of minutes and catches both.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Compare an AI draft with its source and find details that were added or changed.
- Get the tool to list the assumptions it made.
- Correct a draft so it says only what your notes support.
- Apply the same check to a summary of a longer document.

## The trap: a smooth draft with extra facts

Your note said "Tuesday would suit. Not booked." Here's what a tool might write from a thin prompt:

```text
Hi Dave, just confirming you're booked in for 8 am Tuesday to sort
out the yard gate hinge. While you're here, could you also replace
the latch? Cheers.
```

It reads like a normal text. But your note never mentioned a booking, an 8 am start or a latch.

The tool isn't lying on purpose. It writes what usually comes next in a message like this, and contractor messages usually have a confirmed time. These made-up details are often called hallucinations. They're hard to spot because they sound exactly as sure as the true parts.

Send that text and Dave either turns up at 8 am for a job he never agreed to, or rings to ask what you're on about. Either way you're sorting out a mix-up you could have caught before you hit send.

## The check

Do these three things every time, whether it's a two-line text or a two-page summary.

### 1. Compare every date, name, number and promise with your source

Put your notes and the draft side by side. Go through the draft line by line. For each date, time, name, number and promise, find where it came from in your notes. If you can't point to it, it either comes out or becomes a question.

Promises need the closest look. Words like "booked", "confirmed", "as agreed" and "will be done by Friday" turn a question into a commitment, and the other person will hold you to it.

### 2. Ask it to list what it assumed

```text
List every detail in your draft that isn't in my notes, and
anything you assumed.
```

This often turns up the extras quickly. It can miss some too, so treat the list as a head start and still do step 1 yourself.

### 3. Never send it without reading it through

Read the whole draft as the person who'll receive it. Would Dave understand what you're asking? Does it say anything you wouldn't say yourself? It's going out with your name on it.

## Longer documents

The same check works on a summary of a resource consent, a grazing contract or a supply agreement, with one addition. Summaries can drop things as well as add them.

Check the summary against the original. If it says "consent expires in 2031", find that line in the consent. Then ask:

```text
What conditions, dates or obligations in the document did you
leave out of the summary?
```

A missed reporting date or a condition on effluent discharge is easy to lose in a tidy one-page summary, so read the original's conditions section yourself before you rely on it.

## Fixing the draft

Tell the tool exactly what's wrong:

```text
Nothing is booked and there's no agreed time. Remove the booking,
the 8 am start and the latch. Ask whether Tuesday suits.
```

Then check the new version the same way, because a rewrite can bring in a fresh mistake. For a three-line text it's often quicker to fix it yourself.

## Key takeaways

- AI drafts can add details nobody gave them, and those details sound just as sure as the real ones.
- Check every date, name, number and promise against your notes before you use a draft.
- Look hardest at words that turn a request into a commitment, such as booked, confirmed and agreed.
- Ask the tool to list what it assumed, then still do the check yourself.
- For summaries, also ask what was left out.

Treat an AI draft like a text from a keen new worker who's only been on the farm a week. Most of it will be right, and now and then they'll be very sure about something nobody told them. You'd read their message before passing it on, and the same goes here.

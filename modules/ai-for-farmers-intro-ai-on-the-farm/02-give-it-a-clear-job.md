# Give it a clear job

Ask an AI tool to "write a message to the contractor about the gate" and you'll get something polite, vague and quite possibly wrong. Give it the facts and tell it what to do when the facts run out, and you'll get a draft you can send after a quick read.

> **On the farm:** The request you type is called a prompt. A thin prompt gets you a draft you have to rewrite. A clear one gets you a draft you only have to check, and checking is quicker.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- List the five parts of a clear prompt.
- Write a prompt for a short farm message using your own notes.
- Tell the tool what to do when your notes don't cover something.
- Adjust a draft with a follow-up request instead of starting again.

## The five parts of a clear prompt

DairyNZ's advice on AI tools is to be specific and give context, because the answer is only as good as the instructions ([Using Artificial Intelligence on-farm](https://www.dairynz.co.nz/people/productive-workplaces/using-artificial-intelligence-on-farm/)). In practice, a clear prompt covers five things:

| Part | What it covers | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Job | What you want written, and who it's for | A short text to Dave, our fencing contractor |
| Context | Who you are and what's going on | I run a dairy farm, and Dave has worked for us before |
| Facts | The details it must use, taken from your notes | Yard gate hinge is dragging; Tuesday would suit; nothing booked |
| Format | Length, layout and tone | Under 60 words, friendly, plain NZ English |
| Gaps | What to do with anything your notes don't cover | Don't add a time, price or promise; ask instead |

People tend to forget the last part. It does the most to stop the tool filling holes with guesses.

## Worked example: the gate hinge

Here's the note on the fridge at Ridgeback Farm:

```text
Yard gate hinge dragging again. Dave? Tues would suit. Not booked.
```

### A thin prompt

```text
Write a message to my contractor about the gate.
```

The tool has almost nothing to go on, so it fills the blanks with what contractor messages usually say. You might get a day you didn't pick, a time, or "as discussed" when nothing was discussed, and you'd have to find and fix each one.

### A clear prompt

```text
Write a short text message from me to Dave, our fencing contractor.
Context: I run a dairy farm and Dave has done work for us before.
Facts: the hinge on the yard gate is dragging and needs a look.
Tuesday would suit us. Nothing is booked yet.
Format: under 60 words, friendly, plain NZ English.
Gaps: don't add a time, a price or anything Dave has agreed to.
If something is missing, ask Dave rather than guessing.
```

### What a good answer looks like

```text
Hi Dave, the hinge on the yard gate is dragging again. Could you
come and take a look? Tuesday would suit us if you're free. Let
me know what works for you. Cheers.
```

Everything in that reply is in the note. It asks about Tuesday instead of booking it, and there's no time, price or extra job. You can check it in ten seconds and send it.

You don't have to write labelled lines. "Text Dave, our fencing contractor. The yard gate hinge is dragging. Tuesday would suit but nothing's booked, so ask if he's free and don't add a time" does the same job.

## Fixing a near miss with a follow-up

If the reply is close but not quite right, say what to change: "Shorter, and less formal. Mention the gate is by the calf shed." You don't need to start over, because the tool remembers the conversation and works from its last draft.

## Asking it to flag what it guessed

For longer jobs, such as a procedure, a roster or a summary of a contract, add one more line to the end of your prompt:

```text
After the draft, list anything you had to assume or couldn't find
in my notes.
```

That list gives you a head start on checking. The next lesson shows you how to use it.

## Talking instead of typing

The phone apps let you speak your prompt, which is handy in the ute, but pull over first and read the transcript before you send it, because road noise can turn "fifteen" into "fifty".

## Key takeaways

- A clear prompt covers the job, the context, the facts, the format and what to do with gaps.
- Paste the facts from your own notes, because the tool can't see them any other way.
- Tell it what to do when your notes run out, or it will fill the gap with a guess.
- On longer jobs, ask it to list what it assumed.
- Fix a near miss with a follow-up instead of starting again.

You don't need special wording or tricks. Write the prompt the way you'd leave a note for the relief milker: what needs doing, what you know, and what to do if something isn't covered.

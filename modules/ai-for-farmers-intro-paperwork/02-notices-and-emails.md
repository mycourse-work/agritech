# Neighbour notices and emails

Spring thistles are coming away on the Back Flats, and your spray contractor can fit you in mid-October if the wind behaves. The Taylors next door have beehives and a vege garden along that boundary, and they'd rather hear about it before the tractor turns up.

> **On the farm:** A lot of spray complaints start with a neighbour who didn't know it was happening. A clear notice sent in good time keeps that relationship on track, and AI can write one in a minute. The facts in it still have to come from you and your contractor.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Draft a neighbour spray notice that marks every unconfirmed detail as [confirm: …].
- Name who confirms the product, timing, label and notification rules before a notice goes out.
- Write a contractor email that asks questions instead of making promises.
- Describe an animal health concern to your vet as observations, not a diagnosis.

## What the rules expect

NZS 8409:2021 is the New Zealand Standard for managing agrichemicals. [GROWSAFE's notes on the standard](https://www.growsafe.co.nz/Growsafe/Growsafe/AboutUs/NZS8409.aspx) explain that it sets out a spray plan, notification of affected parties and signage when spraying. They also point out that notification rules vary a lot between regions, because regional plans filled gaps in the older standard.

So the details depend on where you farm: who counts as an affected neighbour, how far ahead they need to hear and how you tell them. Check your regional council's plan. Northland Regional Council's [agrichemical spray page](https://www.nrc.govt.nz/environment/air/issues/agrichemical-spray/), for example, says machine and aerial spraying there needs an annual spray plan and a risk assessment on the day.

The split of jobs is simple. AI drafts the wording. The spray operator confirms the product, the timing and the label instructions, and you confirm the notice meets your region's rules.

## Two prompts, two notices

Here's the quick version most of us would type first:

```text
Write a notice to my neighbours that we're spraying the Back Flats next week.
```

It usually comes back polished, with lines like this:

```text
We will be spraying the Back Flats on Tuesday at 7 am using a safe,
low-toxicity herbicide. There is no risk to people, pets or stock.
```

You never said Tuesday or 7 am. Nobody checked whether the product is "safe", and "no risk" is a promise you can't back up. If a neighbour's hives suffer, that sentence is in their letterbox with your farm's name on it.

Now the version that gives the AI the facts and tells it what to do with the gaps:

```text
Draft a friendly letterbox notice to our neighbours about planned spraying.
Farm: Ridgeback Farm, Ridge Road.
Block: the Back Flats, along our boundary with the Taylors.
Planned window: one morning between Tuesday 13 and Thursday 15 October, depending on wind.
Job: ground spraying for thistles with a tractor boom sprayer, done by our contractor.
Contact: Sam on the farm mobile, [number].
Not confirmed yet: product name, start time.
Write anything I haven't given you as [confirm: ...].
Don't add product names, rates or any claim that the spray is safe.
```

A good answer reads like this:

```text
Hi neighbours,

We're planning to spray thistles on the Back Flats, along the boundary
with the Taylors, on one morning between Tuesday 13 and Thursday 15 October.
The day depends on the wind, so it may move.

Our contractor will use a tractor boom sprayer. The product will be
[confirm: product name] and spraying will start at [confirm: start time].

If you have beehives, a vege garden or stock near the boundary, please let
us know before [confirm: date] so we can plan around them.

Any questions, call Sam on [number].

Thanks,
Ridgeback Farm
```

Every gap is visible, so nobody mistakes it for a finished notice. Your contractor fills in the product and start time, and you check your regional rules for who gets it and how early.

> **Important:** Never let a notice say a spray is safe, harmless or low risk. Any advice for neighbours, such as keeping stock away for a time, comes from the product label through your contractor.

## Contractor and vet emails

The same habit works in other emails. With a contractor, ask and don't promise. AI likes to sign off with "See you Tuesday at 7!" when nobody has agreed to anything.

```text
Write a short email to our spray contractor asking:
- which product they plan to use on the Back Flats thistles, and whether they can send the label
- which morning between 13 and 15 October suits them, and what time they'd start
Ask, don't confirm. We haven't booked a day yet.
```

Before you send it, check the draft for a booked day, a time or an agreed price you never gave it.

With a vet, describe what you've seen and let the vet do the diagnosing.

```text
Write a short email to our vet clinic. Six calves in the calf shed have had
loose yellow scours for two days. They're still drinking and standing.
Ask what samples or details the vet wants and whether they'd like to visit.
Describe only what I've told you. Don't name a cause or suggest a treatment.
```

Watch for a draft that says "a likely rotavirus outbreak" or suggests an electrolyte dose. Strike both. The vet needs your observations, not the AI's guesses.

If a calf goes down, stops drinking or you're worried, ring the vet now. Don't wait on an email.

## Key takeaways

- Give the AI your facts and tell it to write [confirm: …] for anything missing.
- Delete any line that says a spray is safe, harmless or low risk.
- The spray operator confirms the product, timing and label. You check your region's notification rules.
- Contractor emails ask questions. They don't book days or times.
- Vet emails describe what you saw. Urgent concerns go to the vet by phone.

A good notice costs you five minutes and saves a lot of awkward conversations over the fence. Let the AI do the wording, and keep the facts for the people who know them.
